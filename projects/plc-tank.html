Three Control Problems, One Simulated Plant

A latch, a sorting line and a tank. I wrote three IEC 61131-3 programs in CODESYS and commissioned each one against a 3D plant in Factory I/O over an OPC DA/UA link. The PLC never talks to the simulator directly, which is exactly how it works when a real controller is brought up against an HMI or SCADA layer. The main project is a process tank where the whole job is analog signal conditioning: turning raw transmitter counts into engineering units, and proving those conversions with numbers from the running plant before any control law goes on top.

The Rig

The PLC never talks to the plant directly.

All three projects use the same toolchain. The program runs in the CODESYS soft-PLC runtime, Factory I/O holds the 3D plant and exposes every sensor and actuator as an OPC tag, and an OPC DA/UA server sits in between. CODESYS publishes the variables in PLC_PRG through a symbol configuration object, the OPC server browses it, and Factory I/O ties each simulated device to one of those items. So the PLC only ever sees the plant as a list of tags, just as it would over a real fieldbus.

That link matters more than it looks. The OPC round trip adds delay on top of the PLC scan, and you can see it in the recordings as a small lag between a tag changing in the watch table and the matching motion in the 3D scene. I wrote the logic to live with that. State is kept in latches and counters instead of being worked out from whatever the sensors happen to read at that instant, and that's the difference between a program that works at one conveyor speed and one that works at any speed.

It's also the reason to simulate the plant in the first place. Getting ladder logic to compile is easy. Getting it to behave with real actuator travel times, sensor debounce and the weight of moving parts is not. A race between a diverter command and a photo-eye, the kind of bug that only shows up at speed, turns up while you're still developing instead of on commissioning day.

Project 07 · The Tank

Four networks and not a single bit of Boolean logic.

Two potentiometers let the operator set the fill and discharge valves, and the level and flow transmitters are scaled into engineering units and shown on two panel displays. Every signal is analog, so all the work is in the scaling: one MOVE, three FC_Scaling blocks and two TO_INT conversions spread across four FBD networks.

Why network 2 is the interesting one

The discharge potentiometer has a centre detent, so it reads −5 at one end and +5 at the other. The valve, like every valve, only goes one way, from 0 to 10. FC_Scaling maps the two-sided range onto the one-sided one:

Tank1_DischargeValve = (Pot2_Discharge − (−5)) / (5 − (−5)) × (10 − 0) + 0 live in FIG 02: Pot2_Discharge = 3.91, so (3.91 + 5) / 10 × 10 = 8.91, which matches

Networks 3 and 4 go the other way. They take the raw 0 to 10 transmitter signal and turn it into the units printed on the tank's gauge board, and then TO_INT converts that to the display's integer type. That conversion rounds instead of chopping off the decimals, and the sampled values show it clearly: a flow of 18.7 displays as 19, and a level of 0.0997 displays as 0.

Verifying the transfer functions against the plant

Scaling parameters are easy to get wrong and impossible to catch just by looking, so I checked both transfer functions with numbers, sampling live values from the online view at three points during the run:

Each FC_Scaling block also drives an Input_Error output. Error1, Error2 and Error3 stay FALSE for the whole recording, which confirms no transmitter ever left its declared range. I count that range check as part of the evidence, not an afterthought.

To be clear

This project is open-loop. There is no PID block, and I'm not claiming one. The operator closes the loop by watching the displays and turning the potentiometers. I see that as the right place to start, not a shortcut. A regulator tuned on a badly scaled transmitter will look perfectly stable and still hold the wrong setpoint. The natural next step is to drive Tank1_FillValve from the error between a setpoint and WaterLevel, leaving the discharge valve as a manual disturbance. The scaling networks would carry over untouched, which is exactly why signal conditioning and control belong in separate pieces.

Project 02 · Seal-In

The smallest piece of memory in ladder logic.

The job is to run a belt conveyor from a two-button station. A quick press of Start has to latch the drive on, letting go must not stop the belt, and Stop has to break the circuit at any moment. It takes six BOOLs and two networks.

C := NOT A AND (B OR C) seal-in latch CC := (AA AND NOT BB) OR (NOT AA AND BB) exclusive OR

Three details carry the whole thing. The C contact in parallel with B feeds the coil's own state back into the rung, so it's the latch that holds the drive, not the button. A sits in series before that parallel block, so holding Start can never override Stop. And A is read through a normally closed contact, the way real panels are wired, so a broken wire to the Stop button cuts power to the rung instead of quietly disabling the stop.

The tag trace proves it. B reads 0 in every sampled frame, because the Start press is only momentary, yet C stays at 1 until A goes high, and then the rung breaks. Meanwhile CC stays 0 the whole time, because AA and BB are both 1, which is the right XOR answer.

Project 05 · Sequencing

By the time the box reaches the junction, the sensors have forgotten it.

A pallet line loads a box, measures how tall it is, sends it down the left or right lane, and counts the boxes on a panel display. It runs on nineteen tags, eighteen BOOLs plus a WORD for the display, driving five RS latches and two CTUD counters.

The height sensors sit before the junction. By the time a pallet reaches the diverter the beams have already cleared, so reading the sensors at the junction would never work. RS_2 latches the result at the moment the box is measured and holds on to it until the transfer is done. It's that latch, not the sensor, that makes the routing work at any conveyor speed.

RS is the reset-dominant bistable, meaning that if Set and Reset both fire in the same scan, Reset wins. That's the safe choice for a sorting station. If a stop or emergency happens at the same moment as a set, the state clears instead of locking a diverter on. I count boxes at the lane exit sensor rather than the entry, so a pallet that gets stuck halfway through a transfer never gets added to the total.

Latching is the building block under everything. The seal-in in Project 02 and the RS blocks in Project 05 are the same idea written two ways, and picking one is mostly about readability. The exception is reset dominance, which RS spells out and a seal contact leaves hidden in the order of the rungs. Sequential logic needs its memory right where the measurement happens, because a decision made metres upstream can't be worked out again at the diverter. Scaling deserves as much attention as control. Most of the work in an analog loop happens before any control law runs, and it can and should be checked with numbers from the live plant. And simulation is what lets me say any of this is correct. Everything on this page is backed by numbers read off the running system, not by a program that simply compiles.
