Ten Stages From a Million Raw Points to "That's a Green Cylinder"

I wanted a robot to look at a messy RGB-D point cloud and say, with some confidence, "there's a green cylinder over there." This ROS 2 package is how I got it to. It listens on /oakd/points, crops and thins the cloud, works out a surface normal for every point, uses RANSAC to throw away the floor, clusters whatever is left, fits cylinders using the geometry of the normals, names each one by its hue, and publishes markers to RViz. Along the way every stage publishes its own debug cloud, so when something broke I could see it instead of guessing.

The Chain

Everything happens inside one callback.

The whole pipeline runs straight through inside a single pointcloud_callback(). One cloud is finished completely before the next one is touched, with no threads and no frame buffer. I kept it that simple on purpose. It meant every frame behaved the same way each time I replayed it, and it let every stage publish exactly what it had produced.

  1. Parse PointCloud2. Unpack xyz and the matching RGB from packed float32 internal
  2. Validity check. Drop NaN and infinite points /stage_valid
  3. Box filter. Keep only the region I care about /stage_box
  4. Voxel downsample. Average the points on a uniform grid /stage_downsample
  5. Normal estimation. k-NN neighborhood and SVD for every point internal
  6. Plane RANSAC. Find the dominant floor plane and remove it /stage_no_plane
  7. Euclidean clustering. Group points by radius with a cKDTree /stage_clusters
  8. Cylinder RANSAC. Fit one cylinder per cluster /stage_cylinders
  9. Semantic labeling. Convert RGB to HSV and sort the hue into red, green, blue or unknown internal
  10. Publish markers. Colored CYLINDER markers /viz/detections

Six of those stages publish a PointCloud2 of their own output. So when a detection came out wrong, I didn't have to theorize about why. I opened RViz, stepped through the stages, and watched for the moment the points stopped making sense.

Geometry

A cylinder axis is just the cross product of two normals.

Cylinder fitting is the most interesting stage, because it samples normals instead of points. The normals come first. For each point I take its k nearest neighbors, build their covariance and run SVD, and the smallest singular vector is the surface normal. After that, one geometric fact does most of the work: on a perfect cylinder, every surface normal is perpendicular to the axis. Pick two normals, take their cross product, and you have a candidate axis straight away, with no least squares involved.

n̂ᵢ = smallest singular vector of cov(k-NN) normal at each point, k = normal_search_k
â = n̂₁ × n̂₂ a candidate axis from two sampled normals
inlier ⇔ d⊥(p,â) ∈ [ r − thresh , r + thresh ] perpendicular distance falls inside the radius band

I score each candidate axis by counting the points whose perpendicular distance lands in the radius band. The best one wins, as long as it clears min_cylinder_inliers. Because the fit runs separately for each cluster, one frame can give several detections instead of one blurry compromise.

Color comes last, and I did it in HSV rather than RGB on purpose. I average the color of the inlier points, convert it, and classify by hue angle alone, so a brightly lit cylinder and one in shadow end up in the same band. Red wraps around the circle (0 to 30° and 330 to 360°), green covers 60 to 180° and blue 180 to 270°. Anything else gets called unknown, because I'd rather the robot admit it isn't sure than guess.

Detections

Three different scenes, one set of parameters.

I tested the pipeline on three recorded RGB-D bags. RViz complains about duplicate markers whenever the number of detections changes from one frame to the next, so every callback sends two messages: a DELETEALL marker first, then the fresh detections.

When a borderline cylinder disappears, I don't twiddle knobs at random. First I raise cluster_dist_thresh so the object stops splitting into two clusters. Then I loosen cyl_radius_thresh. Only after that do I lower min_cylinder_inliers to accept a thinner fit that's still valid. Going in that order means I'm only ever changing one way it can fail at a time.

Looking Back

What I took away from it.

Publishing the intermediate results paid off more than anything else I did. Six debug clouds cost almost nothing, and they changed debugging from guesswork into just looking. It's the difference between "the detection failed" and "the plane RANSAC swallowed the object because it sat almost flat against the floor." I also learned to pick the representation that makes the math easy. Fitting a cylinder to raw points is an optimization problem, but fitting it to normals turns the axis into a cross product. And it pays to classify in whatever space makes the thing you care about a single number. Hue holds up under lighting changes that would break RGB thresholds.
