One UR5, Three Languages, and a Flashlight That Screws Itself Together

I spent this project learning to talk to an industrial cobot at every level it offers: teaching waypoints with PolyScope on the pendant, planning and simulating paths offline in RoboDK, and writing URScript that runs straight on the controller. To prove it all worked, I had the arm trace the eight corners of an imaginary cube with straight-line moves, measured how repeatable it was with a dial indicator, moved blocks between pallets with a ROBOTIQ adaptive gripper, and finished by having it assemble a flashlight, tightening each thread by feeling the torque rather than hoping for the best.

Three Environments

One arm, and three ways to talk to it.

I wasn't after a single demo here. I wanted to be fluent. So I came at every task through the UR5's whole programming stack: PolyScope on the teach pendant to move waypoints live, RoboDK to simulate and plan paths offline around a model of the table, and URScript for programs written as text that run right on the controller, using calls like rq_close_and_wait() for the gripper and get_joint_torques() to read torque. Each one ran the same waypoint missions, so I felt the trade-offs for myself instead of reading them off a datasheet.

Simulate First

Crash it in RoboDK so you don't crash it for real.

I brought the UR5 model into RoboDK along with its table, recorded three targets, and linked them with linear joint-space moves. The program only went to the real robot after the simulated run checked out. I also drove those same three poses through PolyScope twice, once in joint space, setting each joint angle for fine control, and once in Cartesian space, giving pose targets for simpler paths. Doing both made the difference between the two coordinate systems very real.

Cube Tracing

Drawing a cube in thin air, then measuring it.

The motion work ended with the arm tracing all 8 corners of an imaginary cube floating in the workspace. One moveJ brings the arm to the first corner, and every edge after that is a moveL, which forces straight lines in space instead of the curves you get from moving joints. Because I recorded the waypoints, I could then measure the cube from the arm's own path. The diagonal between two corners gives back the side length:

d = √( Δx² + Δy² + Δz² ) = √( 2500 + 2500 + 10000 ) ≈ 122.47 mm  side length from the corner to corner diagonal
V = d³ ≈ 1.80 × 10⁶ mm³  the cube the arm drew, worked out from its own waypoints
Repeatability

Trust the arm, but check it with a dial indicator.

Before I asked the arm to build anything, I measured how precise it really was. I mounted a digital pressure indicator square to a 3D-printed block and had the robot trace the block's edge between waypoints, then its whole face through four corner waypoints, for 10 rounds, reading the indicator every time. The UR5 held its line. The only variation I could see came from the rough printed surface, not from the robot.

Grip & Move

Squeezing just hard enough, and moving blocks without a scratch.

With the ROBOTIQ adaptive gripper I started by opening and closing it by hand on the pendant and worked up to controlling it from scripts, calibrating the grip force in newtons against a load cell at different speed and force settings. The pick-and-place program then moved blocks from one pallet to another using coordinate transforms from the robot's base frame to each pallet. I kept the positions in variables, so a pallet could move without me rewriting the program. As a last test of precision, I had the arm push a 10 mm dowel pin in and out of a row of different-sized holes in a mating plate.

Assembly

Keep screwing until the joints feel 3 N·m.

The last task was building a working flashlight. The arm clamps the head in a pneumatic chuck through Digital I/O, screws the barrel on until get_joint_torques() reads 3 N·m, slides the batteries in the right way round, and tightens the tail cap to its own 2 N·m before unclamping. Tightening by torque rather than by position means the thread itself decides when it's seated, and the robot just has to pay attention.

Looking Back

What I took away from it.

No single programming environment is the right one. PolyScope had the arm moving within minutes, RoboDK caught path problems before they cost me time on the hardware, and URScript did something no GUI could, reading joint torques in the middle of a move and using them to decide when to stop. The real skill is knowing when to drop down a level. I also came to see moveJ and moveL as two different promises. One promises where the joints end up, and the other promises the path in between, and the cube only measured correctly because every edge kept that second promise. And force works as a sensor. The flashlight threads seated properly because the arm was listening to torque, which is the difference between assembling something and confidently cross-threading it.
