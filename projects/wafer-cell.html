Five Machines, Four Languages, One Wafer

A laboratory semiconductor handling cell — a four-axis SCARA, a six-DoF collaborative robot, a stepper conveyor with camera inspection, and an Allen-Bradley Micro820 — moving 100 mm silicon wafers from a stacked cassette, past an inspection station, onto a deposit platform, one shelf lower each cycle. The cell was originally commissioned on different hardware. This is the re-host, and the coordination work the re-host made necessary: a fixture sitting at 393.8 mm of a 400 mm reach where only one inverse-kinematic elbow branch survives the joint limits, an as-built ladder that turns out to differ materially from the one in the manual, and a two-wire handshake designed so that every signal fails in a stated direction.

01 / The Cell

Simple to state. The re-host is the work.

The material flow takes one sentence: a SCARA draws a wafer from a vertical cassette and lays it in a cradle on a conveyor; the belt carries the cradle past a camera that confirms the wafer is there; a collaborative robot lifts it off the cradle and sets it on a deposit platform; the belt returns and the cycle repeats one shelf lower. Every stage is triggered by, and reports back to, a Micro820 PLC. Watch it run end to end:

Reproducing that flow on the hardware actually available turned out to be the substance of the project. Three of the five subsystems are not the ones the original commissioning targeted, and none of the three is a like-for-like substitution.

Four programming environments, no two of them sharing a language. That is characteristic of small-cell integration, and it is exactly why the PLC's role is kept deliberately narrow: it is the only element that sees the whole cell, so it is given signalling and nothing else.

Why a PLC here at all

The justification is determinism of scan, not computing power. A Python script on a PC is subject to the operating system's scheduler, to garbage collection, and to whatever else the machine is doing. A PLC executes its program on a bounded, repeatable scan. The Micro820 is therefore given exactly the jobs that need that property — watching sensor zones, emitting timed trigger pulses, latching done reports, hosting the operator interface — and none of the jobs that do not.

02 / The SCARA

A Cartesian pose does not determine a configuration.

The M1 Pro is a SCARA: two revolute links in the horizontal plane, a vertical lead screw and a wrist. Because the third axis is linear, J3 is Z in millimetres — a convenience with real consequences, since a change in J3 alone is a pure vertical translation that cannot sweep the tool into anything. With L1 = L2 = 200 mm from the dimension drawing, forward kinematics are:

X = L1·cos J1 + L2·cos(J1+J2)
Y = L1·sin J1 + L2·sin(J1+J2)
Z = J3,  R = J1 + J2 + J4 (mod 360°)

The model was validated against live panel data before it was trusted for anything: joint readings J1 = 19.02°, J2 = 29.37°, J4 = −159.79° substituted into those equations return (321.9, 214.7) at R = −111.40°, reproducing the taught rack approach point to the decimal.

Inverting them is where the trouble starts. For a two-link planar arm, a reachable point at radius ρ admits two solutions, distinguished by the sign of J2 — the left and the right elbow. Both are legitimate. The controller's solver picks one, and it does not tell you which.

cos J2 = (ρ² − L1² − L2²) / (2·L1·L2),   J2 = ±arccos(·)

The deposit fixture sits at ρ = 393.8 mm of the arm's 400 mm reach — within 1.5% of full extension. Two things happen there. The Jacobian becomes ill-conditioned, so small Cartesian increments demand large joint excursions and the arm moves in ways that look disproportionate to the command. And, decisively, only one branch stays inside the joint limits. The controller was solving for the other one, so every Cartesian command to the fixture came back "Incorrect motion point for MovJ instruction".

CORRECTIONAn earlier analysis rejected both branches — and was wrong

It treated J4 = −226.3° as beyond the wrist limit. But J4 on the M1 Pro spans ±360°, and −226.3° is the same wrist orientation as +133.7°, because R is defined only modulo 360°. Correcting the limit changes the diagnosis in a way that matters: the point is not unreachable, it is reachable on exactly one branch, and the controller was solving for the other. That also explains an observation the "unreachable" theory could not — the operator can reach the fixture by hand-guiding but not by Cartesian jogging, because hand-guiding moves joints directly and never asks the solver to choose.

Three remedies, one adopted

  • Linear interpolation — rejected. A straight-line move requires every intermediate point to solve, which is strictly more constrained than a joint-interpolated move. It fails harder, not more gracefully.
  • Selecting the configuration explicitly, as the original M1 program did with SetArmOrientation — unavailable. The Lua API exposes no equivalent.
  • Commanding the fixture side in joint coordinates — adopted. A taught joint tuple is a configuration: there is no solve, so there is no ambiguity. The rack side stays Cartesian, because a new Z must be computed for every shelf and parametric Cartesian is the natural way to say that.

The wafer counter increments after J_WITHDRAWN, never after J_DEPOSIT, so an aborted cycle cannot inflate the count.

The taught poses, and what they check

Five poses were taught by hand-guiding and read back from the controller with a probe script rather than transcribed from the pendant, which removes an entire class of digit errors. Each is carried alongside its forward-kinematic Cartesian equivalent, computed by the program at startup — redundancy that costs a few lines and caught two badly-taught poses before they reached the hardware.

  • Every pose has J2 > 0. All five are on branch A, the same branch as the rack side, so the arm never reconfigures anywhere in the cycle. A pose returning branch B was grounds for re-teaching, not for running.
  • Four poses share Y = 363.4 mm. Traverse, approach, deposit and withdrawal lie on one line — which is what a straight fork withdrawal along X should produce. J_WITHDRAWN retracts from X = 134.4 to X = 4.2 mm at unchanged Y, a 130.2 mm translation along the prong axis.
  • Home sits directly above the rack approach point, at (109.6, 352.7), which is why the descent into the cassette is a pure Z move. The factory home was explicitly not used: all-joints-zero puts the arm straight out at X = 400, Y = 0 with the screw below its 5 mm software limit and the fork extended across the bench.

The cassette is the manual's rack, so its internal geometry is a specification rather than a measurement — but the program still refuses to run more than one cycle until the shelf pitch has been confirmed physically, because every cycle after the first depends on it. The measured lift of 13.0 mm is consistent with a 20 mm pitch: it clears the wafer's own shelf lip while staying comfortably below the shelf above.

Guards: refuse, rather than try

  • Untaught poses abort. {0,0,0,0} is not an inert placeholder — it is a real pose, arm straight out at the bottom of the Z screw. Any joint move to an untaught tuple stops the run.
  • Every target is range-checked against the joint limits and the reach/dead-zone envelope before it is issued, so a bad point is reported by name instead of being rejected mid-path by the controller.
  • The run plan is printed and validated before anything moves. A wafer count that would walk past the cassette floor aborts.
  • Staged homing. If the arm is not near home it backs straight out of the cassette corridor first, then rises, then joint-moves home. Rising first would hit the shelf above.
  • On a fault the arm stays put. After an abort it may be somewhere a joint move cannot leave safely, and moving is how a small problem becomes a crash.
03 / Coordination

The ladder in the manual is not the ladder that runs.

The controller is a 2080-LC20-20QBB: twelve digital inputs, seven transistor outputs, four analog inputs, one analog output. Note the two isolated output groups — O-00 through O-03 share CM0 while O-04 through O-06 share CM1 — which is what lets the conveyor-side outputs and the robot-trigger outputs sit in separate reference domains.

The as-built I/O map was recovered from the project's own global variable table and cross-checked against the ladder source. Aliases, not raw addresses, appear on the rungs, and getting them right is most of what makes the ladder readable.

Reproducing the manual's listing would delete the stop input entirely and leave two robot-trigger rungs conducting whether or not the operator had pressed start.

Three choices in this ladder worth naming

  • Pulse timers, not on-delay timers. A TP produces a fixed-width output pulse on a rising input and is immune to what the input does for the rest of that pulse; a TON would track its input and release as soon as the input fell. Since the whole point of these rungs is to emit a trigger of guaranteed width to a robot that samples its input periodically, TP is the correct primitive.
  • Two-second pulses are a deliberate margin. A Micro820 scan is on the order of milliseconds, so a two-second assertion is three orders of magnitude wider than the receiver's sampling uncertainty. No element of this cell needs edge detection. The cost is throughput, and on a laboratory cell that cost is worth paying.
  • Negated conveyor coils mean those outputs idle high and drop low to act, so a loss of power stops the belt rather than running it.

FAULTThe start button did nothing at all, with every other element correct

The panel stop button is NC-wired: its input reads TRUE at rest and FALSE when pressed. That is the fail-safe convention — a cut wire reads as a press — but it means the stop appears as a normally-open ] [ contact on every rung, which looks wrong until you say it aloud: conduct while the stop button is not pressed. Getting it backwards cost one download; rung 1 could not conduct at all, and pressing start did nothing whatever.

GOTCHAThe detection threshold does not survive a download

conveyoron, the comparison value the two analog rungs threshold against, reverts to its default on every project download and must be re-entered by hand. Nothing in the global table is marked retained. The failure is silent: the cell simply never triggers, or triggers continuously. Related, and equally undiagnosable from the symptom: the controller lives at the link-local address 169.254.19.93, so the programming PC's adapter must be on 169.254.x.x / 255.255.0.0 or the Micro820 never appears in the browse tree — presenting as a dead controller rather than as a network misconfiguration.

04 / The Handshake

Two wires carry the entire conversation.

Micro820 O-03 ──▶ opto board ──▶ M1 Pro DI10  "take a wafer"
M1 Pro DO10 ──▶ opto board ──▶ Micro820 I-04  "wafer placed"

Both robot points are on the M1 Pro's second terminal block — the first block's silkscreen only runs to channel 8, which is its own small trap. Expressed as structured text, the rung set is three lines:

(* R1: ask for a wafer *) Scara_in := (Start OR Scara_in) AND Stop AND NOT cycle_done ; (* R2: latch the arm's done report *) cycle_done := (Robot_Done OR cycle_done) AND (Start OR Scara_in) AND Stop ; (* R3: cycle-running indicator *) Light := Scara_in ;

Three properties are load-bearing, and the simulator below runs the real logic so you can break each one:

  • The PLC must drop its request on seeing the done pulse. The arm waits for its start input to fall before considering another wafer, so one long assertion cannot be read as several requests. Hold the request high and the arm finishes moving, then waits forever — indistinguishable, from the outside, from a completed run.
  • The start signal is active HIGH by choice. A dead or disconnected PLC output then reads as no request. Active LOW would read a broken wire as "take another wafer, indefinitely", which is the wrong direction to fail in.
  • The request is not a run permit. The arm samples its start input once, at the top of a wafer. Dropping the request mid-cycle means "do not begin another", and nothing more. Use the E-stop to stop a moving arm.

05 / Transport & Inspection

The same sensor has to mean two different things.

The belt is driven by a stepper through the Dobot Magic Box extended I/O module, which exposes both a motor port and general-purpose digital I/O through the same Python API that drives the arm: one connection, one library, two subsystems. Direction is the sign of the commanded speed and stopping is a commanded speed of zero, so no mechanical changeover is involved. Two infrared photoelectric sensors detect the cradle at each end of travel — both NPN devices with active-low outputs.

Belt motion is governed by the pairing of sensor state with current belt state, not by the sensor alone. That is what lets the same sensor mean "depart" on one pass and "stop" on the return:

TriggerBelt stateAction
Load-end sensor lowstoppedrun forward; arm the camera
Load-end sensor lowreversingstop; ready for next wafer
Pick-end sensor lowforwardstop; cobot picks here
Pick-end sensor lowstoppedrun reverse; return belt

Vision as a presence check, stated as one

Each frame is converted to greyscale, median-blurred to suppress speckle from the belt texture, and run through a circular Hough transform tuned to the wafer's apparent radius. On the first confirmed detection of a cycle the belt stops, a settling delay is allowed, a frame is captured to a sequentially numbered file, and the belt restarts. Two latches make that behave in a continuous loop: a first-run flag suppresses detection until a wafer has actually been loaded, so the transform cannot trigger on circular background objects; an already-photographed flag limits capture to one image per wafer and clears when the load-end sensor fires again. Without them the same wafer is photographed repeatedly while it sits in view, and the belt stops each time.

The transform is used as a presence and gross-geometry check, not as a defect classifier. A genuine inspection function would need controlled illumination and a calibrated scale, and the bench provides neither.

06 / The Cobot

A gripper is a state. A vacuum tool is a circuit.

The Pro600 lifts the wafer off the cradle and sets it on the deposit platform. It is programmed in RoboFlow, a block-structured environment hosted on the controller's own Linux system and reached over remote desktop. The cell program alternates absolute joint moves with tool actuations and two I/O blocks that constitute its entire integration surface: a Wait on digital input 1, and a Set that pulses digital output 1.

The original build used a DH PGE 8 electric parallel gripper with printed finger extensions. This one uses a vacuum tool, and the change is not merely a swap of hardware. A parallel gripper is a two-state device the controller commands directly; the vacuum tool is a pneumatic circuit — filter-regulator, two 24 V solenoid valves for vacuum and blow-off, and a digital vacuum switch whose output is the only evidence that a wafer is actually held. Grip confirmation becomes a sensed quantity rather than an assumed one, which is a strict improvement for a fragile part, at the cost of a compressed-air dependency the gripper did not have.

07 / The Operator Layer

A panel, a supervisory screen, and a count that crosses as edges.

The Micro820 hosts the operator interface as well as the coordination logic: a physical panel with a run switch, four inputs, four output lamps and a 0–10 V analog meter, and a supervisory screen adding start and stop controls, a wafer counter, and live lamps for the start input, both analog sensors, both robot triggers and both conveyor outputs.

The counter deserves a note about representation. On the arm side the wafer count exists as an integer, but the only channel to the PLC is a single discrete output pulsed once per wafer — so what actually crosses the cell boundary is a sequence of edges that the PLC re-integrates. Transferring the count as a number requires a Modbus holding register. The point at which that belongs is marked in the arm program and left as future work.

Familiarisation before anyone touches the panel

Before the cell was patched for real, the trainer panel was modelled in three.js with every terminal and conductor labelled, so the I/O map could be walked through — and argued about — without a live 24 V rail in the room.

Three things that generalise past this bench

  • Redundant representations catch errors that neither catches alone. Every taught joint pose is carried alongside its forward-kinematic Cartesian equivalent, computed at startup rather than transcribed. That caught the traverse pose taught at shelf height, and the mismatch between approach and inserted heights, before either reached hardware. The redundancy costs a few lines.
  • The direction a signal fails in is a design parameter. The start signal is active high so a dead wire means no request; the conveyor outputs idle high so loss of power stops the belt; the stop button is normally closed so a cut wire reads as a press. None of these follows from the sensor convention. Each is a decision about which failure the cell should default to, and each was made explicitly.
  • Signal width should exceed the receiver's sampling uncertainty by a comfortable margin. Two-second pulses against a millisecond scan mean no element of this cell needs edge detection. The cost is throughput; on a production cell the same argument would be made with edge capture and a handshake acknowledgement instead.
09 / What It Does Not Do Yet

Four limitations, stated rather than buried.

  • The deposit fixture is at 393.8 mm of a 400 mm reach. Joint-space commanding makes the configuration deterministic, but it does not improve the conditioning of the workspace at that radius. Moving the fixture 30–40 mm toward the robot is the single change that would most reduce the cell's fragility, and it costs one re-teaching session.
  • Conveyor positioning is open loop. The belt runs until a sensor sees the cradle, and position within the detection window is uncontrolled — which bounds how tightly the cobot's pick pose can be specified. An encoder on the drive, or a second sensor for two-point edge timing, would tighten it.
  • The wafer count crosses the cell boundary as edges, not as a value. A Modbus holding register would carry it as an integer and let the HMI display the arm's own count rather than the PLC's reconstruction of it.
  • Interlocking is procedural rather than enforced. The busy output that would keep the rest of the cell out of the arm's workspace is implemented and wired through the same guarded output helper as everything else, but it is disabled by default because its pin assignment is assumed rather than measured. Enabling it against a confirmed terminal is the first safety improvement worth making.

Beyond those: vision-based wafer alignment, using the inspection camera to correct the cradle pose before the cobot picks, would remove the cell's dependence on the cradle being taped in exactly the taught place. That is the natural next stage.
