Tube-MPC Lateral Path Tracking with CarSim Co-Simulation

Vamsi and I wanted a steering controller that keeps working when the things that usually break controllers show up: disturbances, a model that doesn't match the car, and a steering actuator that runs out of travel. Our answer was Tube-MPC. A nominal MPC plans the center of a tube, and a discrete LQR loop keeps pulling the real vehicle back inside it. We got it working in Simulink first, then took it all the way to a high-fidelity CarSim vehicle.

The Problem

A controller tuned on a clean model is an optimist.

Tune a controller on a tidy linear bicycle model and it will track that model beautifully. Put it on a car with nonlinear tires, different mass, a crosswind and a steering actuator that saturates, and it can fall apart. We saw exactly that in our own benchmarks: an unconstrained LQR under a steady sinusoidal disturbance spent 30.4% of the run in steering saturation and then diverged. So the question we set ourselves was how to keep MPC easy to reason about while giving it a real margin against the unexpected.

Tube-MPC gets there by splitting the job in two. The MPC only has to steer a nominal model that never gets disturbed. A fast feedback loop underneath it deals with the gap between that plan and what the car is really doing, and keeps the true state inside a bounded "tube" around the plan.

Architecture

The whole controller fits in three lines.

uk = vk + sat( tube_on · K · ek )
ek = xk − zk  tube error, the sampled CarSim state minus the nominal state
zk+1 = Ad zk + Bd vk  the discrete nominal model carries the tube center forward
K = [−0.1285  −2.8146  −0.3521  −0.3329]  discrete LQR gain, closed-loop |λ| < 1

The state has four parts, [ey, eψ, vy, r]: lateral error, heading error, lateral velocity and yaw rate. The MPC block puts out the nominal steering vk, the nominal model turns that into the tube center zk, we compare the sampled state xk from the plant or CarSim against it, and the LQR gain K applies the correction. The detail that saved us was saturating that correction before it reaches the steering interface. In the final run the raw correction peaked at 87.8, and feeding that straight in would have thrown the high-fidelity car off the road.

Phase 1 · Fair Benchmark

LQR, MPC and Tube-MPC on the same model and the same disturbance.

Before trusting any of it, we wanted a fair fight. All three controllers ran on the same discrete bicycle model (Ts = 0.02 s), once with nothing going wrong and once with a steady sinusoidal disturbance pushing on the lateral channels.

With no disturbance, MPC and Tube-MPC both reached 0.0016 m RMS, which is 36% better than LQR's 0.0025 m. Verified run, April 2026.

The number I still like best came from simply switching the tube on and off on the Simulink plant. With the feedback loop disabled, the worst error under disturbance was 18.80 m. Turning the tube back on brought it down to 0.0318 m, a 591× reduction, and all it took was a gain vector with four numbers in it.

Phase 2 · CarSim Validation

Then we gave it a proper car.

In the second phase we swapped the simple plant for a CarSim external-driver co-simulation and kept the Tube-MPC signal flow exactly as it was. This is where most of the real work turned out to be: unit conversions, the steering ratio, mapping the yaw frame, building the four-state vector out of CarSim's outputs, and getting the saturation in the right place. The final 30 s run at 54 km/h finished closed-loop with the tube errors staying bounded (e₁ max 0.455, e₃ max 0.324) and a peak steering wheel demand of 45.8°. We saved everything needed to reproduce it: the MAT runs, summary_metrics.csv, the plot scripts and the visualizer animation.

Looking Back

What I took away from it.

The robustness came from how the controller was put together, not from how hard we turned the weights. Splitting planning from correction did more than any amount of tuning. Constraints also turned out to decide whether a controller degrades gracefully or falls over: the disturbance LQR couldn't handle left both constrained controllers perfectly stable. And moving from a simple simulation to a high-fidelity one is its own kind of engineering. Once CarSim was in the loop, signal frames, unit conversions and where the saturation sat mattered just as much as the control law did.
