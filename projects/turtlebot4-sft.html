SmartFollower & Tracker: A TurtleBot 4 That Keeps You in Frame

A human-following system for GPS-denied warehouse environments: an OAK-D camera detects an ArUco board carried by the target, solvePnP recovers its 6-DoF pose, and a tracking filter — Kalman and 300-particle implementations, benchmarked head-to-head on the same hardware run — feeds velocity commands through a three-state FOLLOW / PREDICTED / LOST supervisor with a LiDAR safety guard. Built sim-first in Gazebo, then validated on a physical TurtleBot 4.

The Mission

Follow the inspector. Don't hit anything. Don't lose them.

The scenario is warehouse anomaly investigation: a mobile robot that can detect, track, and safely follow a designated object of interest through a dynamic, GPS-denied logistics floor — aisles, staging areas, packaging zones — while circumventing obstacles and logging time-stamped evidence for later reconstruction. The environment fights back with everything indoor robotics hates: occlusions, lighting variability, narrow passages, and people who move like people.

The team wrote the requirements as numbers before writing any code: target acquisition in ≤ 3 s, tracking continuity ≥ 90% over five-minute runs containing at least three occlusion events, re-acquisition within seconds of a loss, a regulated follow distance, and zero collisions — ever.

Platform

A TurtleBot 4, asked to earn its sensors.

The platform is a TurtleBot 4 on a Create 3 differential-drive base: wheel encoders and IMU for odometry, a 2D LiDAR for obstacle detection and mapping, an OAK-D RGB-D camera for target identification and depth-based ranging, and a Raspberry Pi doing the thinking. The base's bump, cliff, and wheel-drop sensors form a hardware safety floor beneath everything the software decides.

ẋ = v·cos(θ)    ẏ = v·sin(θ)    θ̇ = ω  // differential-drive model, state [x, y, θ]ᵀ
u = [v, ω]ᵀ  // the only two knobs the controller gets to turn

The target carries an ArUco fiducial board (DICT_5X5_100 dictionary, 2.25 cm markers) rather than relying on appearance-based person detection — a deliberate trade: fiducials give robust identity and metric relative pose under exactly the occlusion and lighting variability the warehouse setting guarantees.

Architecture

Three nodes: see, believe, act.

The ROS 2 pipeline runs at 20 Hz across three custom nodes. A perception node detects the ArUco board with adaptive thresholding and corner validation, then solves for full 6-DoF pose. A tracking node maintains a filtered estimate of where the target is — and where it's going — even between detections. A control node turns the tracked state into velocity commands, with a LiDAR safety guard that can veto anything the follow law proposes.

t = [x, y, z]ᵀ = solvePnP(corners, board geometry)  // pixels → meters, one function that must never lie
// z: forward distance to target   x: lateral offset — the two numbers that drive the robot
Tracking · KF vs PF

Two filters walk into a warehouse.

The camera loses the board constantly — a shoulder turn, a shelf edge, a lighting flare — so the tracker is what actually holds the system together. The team built two interchangeable estimators behind the same interface and raced them. The Kalman filter carries state [x, z, vx, vz]ᵀ under a constant-velocity model: predict forward with velocity, correct with each new measurement, weighted by the Kalman gain. The particle filter scatters 300 hypotheses with Gaussian process noise, weights them by measurement likelihood, and systematically resamples — more expressive for nonlinear motion, and far hungrier for tuning.

Both expose the same predict-correct API — update() on each measurement, peek(dt) for a single-step forecast, rollout(dt, n) for visualizing predicted paths — so the control node genuinely cannot tell which one is running. That's what made the benchmark honest.

Control & Supervision

Follow at 0.65 m. Trust decays in three seconds.

Above the control law sits a three-state supervisor. While the board is visible — MEASURED — the robot follows at full authority. Lose it, and the system drops to PREDICTED: for up to three seconds it follows the filter's forecast at reduced speed, coasting through the occlusion on physics rather than sight. Past three seconds it declares LOST and stops — a robot that keeps driving on a stale guess is a robot that finds the one obstacle the spec said it never could. The LiDAR guard runs underneath all three states.

Sim First

Break it in Gazebo, where crashes are free.

The full pipeline ran in Gazebo simulation before it ever touched hardware — a leader TurtleBot carrying the board through a walled test room while the follower tracked it, with RViz mirroring the occupancy grid, laser scan, and camera view. Same nodes, same topics, same parameters: the sim-to-real port was a camera calibration and a TF tree, not a rewrite.

Results

The Kalman filter didn't just win. The particle filter left the map.

Both backends replayed the same recorded 20 Hz hardware run — identical detections, identical timing — so the only variable was the filter. The KF held near-zero error for the entire trial. The PF tracked cleanly for about twelve seconds, then diverged: its lateral estimate began oscillating across ±1.5 m and its forward estimate railed against the 3.0 m safety clamp for the rest of the run.

The verdict was unambiguous: for a human-speed target, the constant-velocity assumption is nearly exact, and the Kalman filter is the right tool — numerically sound, cheap, and stable. The PF's flexibility bought nothing here but variance.

Takeaways

What this project proves.

Benchmark filters behind one interface, or you're benchmarking your glue code. Because KF and PF were drop-in swaps on identical replayed data, the 12-second divergence was indisputably the filter's — no confound survived. Match the model to the motion: a human walking is a constant-velocity process, and 300 particles of extra generality lost to four state variables of the right assumption. And degrade on a timer, not on hope — three seconds of prediction, then stop. The state machine's honesty about what the robot doesn't know is what kept the zero-collision record intact.
