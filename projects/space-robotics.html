Balancing a Pendulum Through a Simulated Earthquake

An underactuated cart-pole — one actuator, four states, and a continuous earthquake force generator trying to knock it over. The task was to design and tune a Linear Quadratic Regulator that keeps the pole upright and the cart inside a ±2.5 m rail, then prove it with logged metrics rather than a video that looks stable. Tuning Q and R turned a controller that flung the cart 3 m and saturated at ~300 N into one holding 0.09 rad of pole deviation and 7.8 cm RMS cart error at a 70 N peak.

The Plant

Four states, one actuator, and a ground that won't hold still.

The cart-pole is the canonical underactuated system: the state is [x, ẋ, θ, θ̇] — cart position and velocity, pole angle and angular rate — but the only input is a horizontal force on the cart. There is no torque at the pole joint. Every correction to the pole has to be bought by moving the cart, and the cart has only ±2.5 m of rail to spend. That coupling is the entire difficulty: aggressively fixing the angle drives the cart into its limit, and protecting the cart lets the pole fall.

What makes it more than a textbook exercise is the disturbance. An earthquake generator injects continuous force built from a superposition of sine waves — 15 N base amplitude across 0.5–4.0 Hz, with randomized amplitude and phase plus Gaussian noise on top. It's broadband, persistent, and never repeats, so the controller can't settle into any steady state; it's always rejecting.

A = [[0,1,0,0], [0,0, mg/M, 0], [0,0,0,1], [0,0, (M+m)g/(ML), 0]]
B = [0, 1/M, 0, −1/(ML)]ᵀ  // M = m = 1.0 kg, L = 1.0 m
K = solve_continuous_are(A, B, Q, R) → u = −Kx // control loop at 100 Hz

The controller linearizes about the upright equilibrium, solves the continuous algebraic Riccati equation for the optimal gain, and applies u = −Kx. Which means the real design work isn't writing the controller at all — it's choosing what optimal means, and that lives entirely in Q and R.

Tuning

Q and R are where the engineering judgment goes.

LQR minimizes ∫(xᵀQx + uᵀRu) dt — Q prices deviation in each state, R prices control effort. The starting weights treated cart position as barely worth anything (Q[0,0] = 1.0) and only mildly penalized pole angle (Q[2,2] = 10.0), with cheap control (R = 0.1). The result was exactly what those prices ask for: a controller that happily slides the cart 3 m and slams in ~300 N of force to chase the angle, because neither was expensive.

WeightBeforeAfterIntent
Q[0,0] — cart position1.050.0make displacement expensive → stay on the rail
Q[1,1] — cart velocity1.05.0smooth the cart, kill erratic darting
Q[2,2] — pole angle10.0100.0correct tilt harder — the primary objective
Q[3,3] — pole rate10.020.0damp fast oscillation before it builds
R — control effort0.10.1held: force headroom needed for 4 Hz disturbance

The key move is the 50× increase on cart position alongside the 10× on pole angle. Raising only the angle weight makes the pole better and the displacement worse; raising both in proportion is what buys stability in one without sacrificing the other. Q[3,3] doing damping duty is the quiet one — penalizing angular rate stops oscillations while they're small, which costs far less force than correcting them once they're large.

Results

Logged, not eyeballed.

A dedicated performance_metrics node logs the numbers that matter while the earthquake runs — peak pole deviation, RMS cart error, peak force, control effort efficiency, recovery time, and the achieved control rate. Two runs from that node tell the tuning story better than any plot: an intermediate configuration, and the final one.

Logged metricIntermediate runFinal tuning
Max pole angle deviation0.1520 rad0.0900 rad (5.2°)
RMS cart position error0.2266 m0.0779 m
Peak control force104.27 N70.12 N
Control effort efficiency58.29%71.95%
Average recovery time0.013 s0.030 s
Control rate67.83 Hz43.05 Hz

Against the untuned baseline the improvement is larger still: peak force fell from around 300 N to 70.12 N, and maximum cart excursion from roughly 3.1 m — outside the physical rail — to about 1.1 m, comfortably inside it. The honest caveat is the last row: the final configuration logs a lower control rate, so there's a real trade between how hard the gains work and how fast the loop closes.

Rocky Times Challenge

Find two rocks, measure them, land on the tall one.

The course capstone flew a PX4 SITL quadrotor with a downward camera and RGB-D sensing into an unknown scene holding two cylindrical rock formations — 10 m and 7 m tall. The mission: search the area, locate both, estimate their diameters and world-frame positions, and land on top of the taller one, all while logging time and energy.

The search strategy is the first real decision, and it's a coverage-versus-information trade. A spiral is efficient when the target is likely central; random walks suit total uncertainty; frontier-based exploration is optimal when a SLAM map is being built live; a grid + TSP hybrid wins in cluttered semi-known space. For a bounded arena with two guaranteed targets the answer is the boring one — a lawnmower sweep with line spacing set from the camera's field of view gives provable complete coverage, and its straight-line legs keep the airframe stable enough to hand off to a precision descent. Same boustrophedon idea as the turtlesim assignment, now in three dimensions with a real vehicle under it.

TAKEOFF → SURVEY (lawnmower, abort on detection) → GOTO_ARUCO → HOVER → DESCEND → LAND
marker pose: OpenCV ArUco → solvePnP(intrinsics) + PX4 odometry → world position
control: /fmu/in/offboard_control_mode · /fmu/in/trajectory_setpoint · /fmu/in/vehicle_command

Detection runs on the downward mono stream against published camera intrinsics; when a marker appears mid-survey the remaining sweep is abandoned immediately, the marker's global position is inferred from solvePnP's translation vector composed with onboard odometry, and the vehicle walks in on position setpoints before hovering to stabilize and descending. Mission parameters: 0.8 m marker, 15 m takeoff altitude, 2.0 m survey step, 0.2 m final descent height. RTAB-Map covered the 3D reconstruction extension, exporting both rocks as a mesh.

The instructive part is the failure. Across trials the pipeline ran clean — coverage, detection, hover, descent — but touchdown landed consistently beside the marker rather than on it, and consistency is the tell. A random scatter would suggest noise; a repeatable offset in the same direction points at a systematic error: frame misalignment between camera and body, the tvec from solvePnP mapped into the world frame with a wrong transform, or depth estimates drifting during descent as the marker fills the frame. Fixing that is a calibration and TF problem, not a control-gain problem — which is exactly the kind of diagnosis worth being able to make.

Takeaways

What this project proves.

Optimal control is a pricing problem. The LQR machinery is four lines of scipy; everything that determined whether this thing stayed on the rail was the relative cost assigned to displacement, tilt, and effort. Getting Q wrong doesn't produce a broken controller — it produces a controller that optimally does the wrong thing. Underactuated means every fix costs something elsewhere: there is no gain that improves pole angle for free, only a trade you choose deliberately. And instrument before you tune — the metrics node is what turned "looks stable" into 0.0900 rad and 70.12 N, which is the difference between a claim and a result.
