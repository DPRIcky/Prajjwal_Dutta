Three Halves of an Autonomous Robot

Every mobile robot keeps asking itself three things: what's out there, where am I, and how do I get there without wasting energy. In RAS 598 I answered each one myself, without leaning on nav2, pcl_ros or amcl. The result is three separate ROS 2 packages. I wrote every algorithm in them by hand: RANSAC on point normals, a histogram filter over nearly half a million belief cells, and A* with Bresenham line-of-sight pruning.

The Stack

Start wherever you like.

Each package has its own repository, its own simulation setup and its own results. I've put them in the order a robot would actually use them.

01 · Perception

OAK-D Cylinder Perception Pipeline

Ten stages that turn raw RGB-D point clouds into labeled objects: a box filter, voxel downsampling, k-NN and SVD normals, RANSAC plane removal, Euclidean clustering, cylinder fitting from cross products of normals, and HSV color labels. Every stage publishes its own debug cloud so I could see exactly where things went wrong.

10 stages6 debug topics50 to 200 ms/frame
github.com/DPRIcky/perception-cylinder-pipeline
02 · Localization

3D Discrete Bayes Filter Localization

A histogram filter that keeps the whole posterior over (x, y, θ) in an 80×80×72 array. It predicts by breaking odometry into turn, go and turn again with Gaussian blur, and corrects with range and bearing to landmarks. Two or three sightings get it within 0.6 m, and when I kidnapped the robot it failed in a way that taught me something.

460,800 cells0.6 m convergenceσ_r 0.5 m · σ_b 15°
github.com/DPRIcky/bayes-filter-robot-localization
03 · Planning

Energy-Aware A* Motion Planning

A* on a carefully downsampled 80×80 grid, greedy pruning with Bresenham line of sight, corner checks on diagonals, and a Turn-Go-Turn controller tuned to save energy rather than time. Planning got 67× faster, the path lost 89.8% of its waypoints, the robot used about 90% less energy, and 34 unit tests cover every layer.

Common Ground

The same cave, seen three ways.

Localization and planning run in the same place: a 16 × 16 m cave in the Stage simulator, at the same 0.2 m grid resolution. They also share the Turn-Go-Turn way of breaking up motion. In one package it's the motion model that predicts where the robot went, and in the other it's the controller that decides how to move. That was the most useful thing I learned from doing all three. The same idea helps you guess where you ended up and helps you plan how to get somewhere.

Perception lives in sensor space instead, working on OAK-D RGB-D frames played back from recorded rosbags. The habits carried over though. I published every intermediate result, kept all the tuning in one config file, and checked the output against something more solid than a screenshot.
