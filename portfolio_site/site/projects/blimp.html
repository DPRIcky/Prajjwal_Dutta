AeroFusion: An Autonomous Blimp, from Custom Airframe to Vision Autonomy

A lighter-than-air UAV that finds, tracks, and approaches balloon targets on its own: a YOLOv5 detector feeding quadrant-based PI control, a Kalman filter fusing 200 Hz IMU and barometric altitude, and thrust allocation through a pseudo-inverse motor map — all running onboard a Raspberry Pi. Built as a three-person course team, then carried forward as the AMASS Lab's Defend-the-Republic competition platform, where I lead the software stack.

The Mission

Fly slower than a drone. Think harder than a balloon.

The team's research question: how effectively can sensor data fusion enhance control and tracking of hybrid robotic blimps in dynamic environments? A blimp is a maddening control plant — buoyancy-dominated, underactuated, pushed around by air currents a quadcopter would never notice, with actuators that whisper rather than shout. The mission profile, drawn from the Defend-the-Republic competition format: autonomously detect a balloon target, steer toward it, and capture it — with every sensor and every inference cycle running onboard.

That constraint shaped everything. No motion-capture crutch, no offboard GPU: a Raspberry Pi, a camera, an IMU, and a barometer had to carry detection, state estimation, and control simultaneously — in real time.

Platform

Every gram argued for its seat.

The airframe is a custom SolidWorks design: an ellipsoidal helium envelope with a 3D-printed, foam-and-balsa gondola slung at the center of gravity. Onboard: a Raspberry Pi 4B (8 GB) with a custom power-distribution PCB, a BNO085 IMU at 200 Hz, a BMP390 barometer at 50 Hz for altitude, and a Pi Camera v2 streaming 640×480 at 30 fps. Thrust comes from a differential pair of side motors plus vertical thrust for heave, driven through ESCs at 50 Hz PWM — all fed by a single 7.4 V, 900 mAh LiPo.

Architecture

Sense. Fuse. Decide. Actuate — at 50 Hz.

eu = uc − 320  // pixel yaw error: detection centroid vs frame center
ψcmd = Kp,ψ·eu + Ki,ψ·∫eu dt  // vision-based yaw PI
fz = Kp,z·ez + Ki,z·∫ez dt  // barometric altitude PI
u = T†( Fdesired − Fbuoyancy )  // pseudo-inverse thrust allocation → 4× PWM

The ROS 2 stack is layered: a sensor layer publishing three asynchronous streams (200 Hz IMU, 50 Hz barometer, ~30 fps detections); an estimation layer where a Kalman filter fuses them into a 12-dimensional state — 6-DoF pose plus linear and angular velocities — recovering the derivatives no sensor measures directly; a control layer running the PI laws above; and an actuation layer converting desired forces to individual motor commands through the pseudo-inverse of the thrust-allocation matrix, saturated to ESC bounds. A mode switcher arbitrates between joystick manual control, full autonomy, and safety overrides. If the detector loses the balloon, the blimp holds barometric altitude and stops yawing — hover, don't guess — until re-detection.

Vision Autonomy

A quadrant is all the geometry you need.

A YOLOv5s model, trained on a custom balloon dataset and quantized to 8-bit for CPU inference, reached ~96% mAP@0.5 — about 20 ms per frame on GPU and under 100 ms on CPU. Rather than full 3D target estimation, navigation uses the detection centroid directly: the image is split into four quadrants plus a center deadband, and each region maps to an intuitive command pair.

Dynamics · Validated on Vicon

A blimp is an underwater vehicle that got lucky.

Buoyancy-dominated flight means quadrotor models don't apply — so the team derived a full 6-DoF lighter-than-air model instead: rigid-body mass and inertia extracted from the CAD, added-mass terms computed via Lamb's k-factors for the ellipsoid, plus Coriolis, damping, and gravity–buoyancy restoring terms. Single-axis thrust tests tracked with Vicon motion capture validated the model: theoretical and experimental trajectories align in both forward and vertical motion, confirming that LTA vehicle dynamics follow the same structure as underwater-vehicle models — with buoyancy doing the work water usually does.

Beyond the Course

From class project to competition platform.

The blimp outlived the semester. It became the AMASS Lab's Defend-the-Republic competition platform, with a ten-person team building on the stack — and me owning software architecture and integration as the primary code reviewer. The 51-page technical handoff we published covers reproducible bring-up from a clean OS image, full mechanical and electronics BOMs, wiring and pin maps, and system-level acceptance tests with explicit pass criteria and archived evidence — logs, plots, and videos pinned to commits.

Takeaways

What this project proves.

Autonomy budgets are physical. Every design call — YOLOv5s over larger models, quadrant logic over 3D estimation, IMU+barometer over GPS — traded capability for grams, milliseconds, and milliamps, and the system flew because those trades were made honestly. Fusion is what makes cheap sensors act expensive: no single sensor onboard could localize the blimp, but the Kalman-fused stack held autonomous lock on a moving target. And platforms outlive projects — documentation, acceptance tests, and clean handoff turned one semester's build into a lab's ongoing competition program.
