Plan on a Coarser Map, Throw Away 90% of the Waypoints, Then Drive Slowly

A complete ROS 2 navigation stack built around a single question: what actually costs a mobile robot energy? The answer drove every decision — plan on a downsampled grid so search is cheap, prune the path with Bresenham line-of-sight so the robot stops turning, and drive it with a Turn-Go-Turn state machine at conservative velocity. Result: planning 67× faster, waypoints cut by 89.8%, and roughly 90% energy savings against baseline — with 34 unit tests holding it in place.

Resolution

The map you store and the map you search are different maps.

The occupancy grid is a 500 × 500 PNG at 0.032 m/pixel over a 16 × 16 m world — good detail, terrible for search: 250,000 cells, ~183,000 A* expansions, roughly ten seconds to plan. Planning directly at the required 0.2 m resolution would be fast but throws away obstacle detail. The resolution is instead treated as a downsampling problem: keep the fine map, search a coarse one derived from it via cv2.resize with INTER_NEAREST.

The interpolation choice is the whole trick. Any averaging kernel would blur an obstacle boundary into a half-occupied cell and invent passages that don't exist. Nearest-neighbor preserves hard boundaries, so a wall stays a wall at 0.2 m.

Search & Prune

A* finds a path. Line-of-sight makes it worth driving.

The global planner is A* with 8-directional movement, a Euclidean heuristic (admissible and consistent, so the result is optimal on the grid), and a priority queue for O(n log n) node selection. What it returns, though, is a staircase — 108 waypoints that zigzag one cell at a time because that's how grid neighbors work. Driving that literally means turning constantly, and turning is where a differential robot burns its energy.

So the path is pruned greedily: from the current waypoint, find the farthest one still visible, jump straight to it, repeat. Visibility is tested with Bresenham's line algorithm rather than sampled interpolation — Bresenham guarantees every grid cell the line touches gets checked, so a shortcut can't slip diagonally through a wall between two samples. 108 waypoints become 11.

pruned = [path[0]]; i = 0
while i < len(path)−1:
  farthest = i+1
  for j in range(i+2, len(path)):
    if bresenham_line_of_sight(path[i], path[j]): farthest = j
    else: break // hit an obstacle — stop extending
  pruned.append(path[farthest]); i = farthest

Three more collision layers back that up: cell-level occupancy during neighbor expansion, a 0.65 m inflation radius around obstacles (spec required > 0.6 m), and diagonal corner checking — a diagonal step from (x, y) to (x±1, y±1) is only legal if both orthogonal neighbors are clear, which stops the planner from squeezing through the zero-width gap where two walls meet at a corner.

Control

Turn, go, turn — decoupled on purpose.

The local controller is a three-state machine: IDLE waits for a task, ROTATE turns in place until heading error is under 0.15 rad, and DRIVE moves toward the waypoint until it's within 0.2 m. Separating rotation from translation costs a little time and buys precision — the robot never tries to correct heading and position simultaneously, which is what produces the arcing overshoot that then needs correcting again.

ω = clamp(1.5 · heading_error, −0.4, 0.4) rad/s // proportional, clamped for stable turns
v = 0.25 · (1 − min(dist, 1.0)) m/s // ramps down approaching the waypoint

Energy savings were budgeted, not hoped for. Pruning contributes the bulk at ~60% — every eliminated waypoint is an eliminated stop-and-turn. Smooth state transitions add ~25%, the conservative 0.25 m/s cap another ~10%, and distance-based velocity scaling the last ~5%, for roughly 90% total against the naive baseline. Actual consumption is tracked live on /energy_consumed from the grading scout, so the claim is measured rather than modeled.

Verification

34 tests, because "it looked fine in RViz" isn't evidence.

Each layer is tested where it can actually fail. Map loading covers the coordinate transforms in both directions plus inflation. A* is tested for a basic path, a complex path, an unreachable goal, and optimality. Pruning is tested for straight-line reduction, obstacle avoidance, a single waypoint, and the case where no improvement is possible. The controller gets 13 tests — angle wrapping, threshold behavior, velocity scaling, goal arrival — and energy optimization gets 8, including straight-versus-zigzag and a realistic full mission.

Takeaways

What this project proves.

Optimal on the grid isn't optimal to drive. A* returns the shortest cell path, but cell paths are staircases, and a staircase is a turn schedule — pruning is where an optimal path becomes an efficient one. Pick the algorithm whose guarantee matches the risk: interpolated collision sampling usually works and occasionally clips a corner; Bresenham can't, because covering every touched cell is exactly what it's defined to do. And energy is a design budget, not a byproduct — decomposing the ~90% into pruning, state transitions, velocity cap, and scaling is what turned "drive efficiently" into four decisions that could each be tested.
